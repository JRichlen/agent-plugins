# Phase structure prior art

**Status:** research note for Red Gate (`docs/red-gate-protocol.md`).
**Question:** Red Gate runs every round as exactly 3 stages — arm the verifier (criteria proven able to fail, then pinned), do the work (one tracer slice), independent judge (the pinned verifier run by a party that did not do the work). Variance in the *kind* of work lives in round types (orientation/scout, plan, build, consolidation/widen), not in extra stages. Should the protocol add per-round phases such as Reflect, Plan, or Investigate — or keep the 3-stage skeleton and push that variance into round types, gate obligations, and cross-round mechanisms?
**Method:** each prior-art loop below was checked against a primary or canonical source (original arXiv paper, the author's own canonical write-up, the official guide, or the book text). For each: (a) phases per iteration, (b) whether reflection/investigation is a phase inside *every* iteration, a separate iteration *type*, or a *cross-iteration* mechanism, (c) citation. Ambiguity and inference are labeled as such.

---

## Classical improvement and problem-solving cycles

### 1. PDCA / PDSA — Shewhart cycle, Deming cycle

**(a)** Four steps per cycle: Plan, Do, Study (Deming) or Check, Act.
**(b)** The reflective step (Study/Check) is *inside* every cycle — but it is the analogue of Red Gate's judge stage, not a free-floating "reflect" phase: the Plan step "involves identifying a goal or purpose, formulating a theory, defining success metrics," and Study "monitors outcomes to test the validity of the plan." That is arm-the-verifier followed by run-the-verifier. The fourth step, Act, is explicitly *cross-cycle*: per the Deming Institute, "The Act step closes the cycle, integrating the learning generated by the entire process, which can be used to adjust the goal, change methods, reformulate a theory altogether, or broaden the learning-improvement cycle from a small-scale experiment to a larger implementation Plan" ([deming.org/explore/pdsa](https://deming.org/explore/pdsa/)). Act does not add work inside the current cycle; it feeds adjusted theory into the *next* cycle's Plan — including a widen move ("broaden ... to a larger implementation") that corresponds to Red Gate's consolidation/widen round type. The Institute also stresses Deming's PDSA-not-PDCA distinction: Check is pass/fail on a plan, Study is comparing predicted vs. actual results to revise the theory — i.e., the reflective content Deming cared about is *theory revision between cycles*, guided by pre-declared predictions, not in-cycle rumination.
**(c)** W. Edwards Deming Institute, "PDSA Cycle," https://deming.org/explore/pdsa/ (quotes above verbatim). The Institute credits the cycle to Walter Shewhart of Bell Laboratories.
**Reading for Red Gate (inference):** PDSA is a 4-label rendering of the same content Red Gate spreads across 3 stages plus a cross-round mechanism: Plan ≈ arm (metrics defined up front), Do ≈ work, Study ≈ judge, Act ≈ what the gate does with a verdict — carry learning into the next round or widen. Act is not evidence for an in-round Reflect phase; it is evidence for a cross-round adjustment obligation.

### 2. OODA — Boyd

**(a)** Nominally four: Observe, Orient, Decide, Act.
**(b)** OODA is *not* a per-action sequential procedure in Boyd's own final formulation. Boyd's only canonical statement is the five-slide 1995/96 briefing "The Essence of Winning and Losing," preserved by Chet Richards and archived with the OODA "Loop" sketch at danford.net ([danford.net/boyd/essence.htm](http://www.danford.net/boyd/essence.htm)). The sketch (slide 4) shows Orientation exerting "implicit guidance and control" directly over both Observation and Action — i.e., action can flow from orientation without a discrete Decide step, and all four activities run *simultaneously and continuously*, cross-referencing each other, rather than ticking around a circle. (This reading of the sketch is standard — the Wikipedia article notes "The loop includes continuous collection of feedback and observations" and contrasts OODA's late commitment with PDCA's early commitment, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OODA_loop — but I could not extract the slide text itself through the proxy, so the sketch description is paraphrase of the archived primary, not quotation.) Observe/Orient are therefore a *continuous background posture*, closest to Red Gate's orientation/scout round type plus whatever standing context the gate maintains — not an investigation phase bolted inside each act. Honest caveat: OODA is also the loosest source here; aviation historian Michael Hankins (quoted in the same Wikipedia article) calls it "vague enough that its defenders and attackers can each see what they want to see in it," so it under-determines any structural conclusion.
**(c)** John R. Boyd, "The Essence of Winning and Losing" (1995), archived at http://www.danford.net/boyd/essence.htm; overview and criticism at https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OODA_loop.

### 3. Polya, *How to Solve It* (1945)

**(a)** Four phases per problem: Understand the problem, Devise a plan, Carry out the plan, Look back.
**(b)** Looking Back is prescribed per problem but Polya is candid that it is honored mostly in the breach, and his own text splits it into two different things. The verification half is exactly a judge stage: "errors are always possible, especially if the argument is long and involved. Hence, verifications are desirable ... Can you check the result? Can you check the argument?" The consolidation half is cross-problem learning: "Even fairly good students, when they have obtained the solution of the problem and written down neatly the argument, shut their books and look for something else. Doing so, they miss an important and instructive phase of the work. By looking back at the completed solution ... they could consolidate their knowledge and develop their ability to solve problems," and "Can you use the result, or the method, for some other problem?" (all quotes from Part I, §13, "Looking back," full text at https://www.hlevkin.com/hlevkin/90MathPhysBioBooks/Math/Polya/George_Polya_How%20to%20Solve%20It.pdf). Polya also warns the phases are not rigid: "the divisions between phases are not rigid, and it is important to be flexible in one's approach" (summarized with quotes at https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/How_to_Solve_It).
**(c)** G. Polya, *How to Solve It*, Princeton University Press, 1945; PDF above; phase summary at https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/How_to_Solve_It.
**Reading (inference):** the part of Looking Back that must not be skipped — checking result and argument — is what Red Gate's judge stage already mandates. The part students actually skip — consolidation, transfer to other problems — is the aspirational cross-problem mechanism, and Polya frames it as teacherly exhortation ("A good teacher should ... impress on his students"), not a gate. A protocol that wants consolidation to happen reliably should make it a gated round type, which is what Red Gate's consolidation round is.

### 4. Kolb's experiential learning cycle (1984)

**(a)** Four stages: Concrete Experience, Reflective Observation, Abstract Conceptualization, Active Experimentation.
**(b)** Reflection is a stage inside every cycle — this is the strongest prior-art case *for* an in-loop Reflect phase. But the cycle's unit is *learning from an experience*, not *producing verified work*: "Learning is the process whereby knowledge is created through the transformation of experience" (Kolb, 1984, p. 38, quoted at https://www.simplypsychology.org/learning-kolb.html). Kolb's cycle has no verification stage at all — nothing in it checks that the work was correct; it optimizes what the learner takes away. In Red Gate terms the whole Kolb cycle is the *cross-round learning mechanism* (what the gate should extract from a finished round), not a template for the round itself. Caveat: source used is a canonical secondary summary of Kolb (1984), *Experiential Learning*; the p. 38 quote is verbatim, the structural reading is mine.
**(c)** D. Kolb, *Experiential Learning: Experience as the Source of Learning and Development*, 1984; summary with quotes at https://www.simplypsychology.org/learning-kolb.html.

### 5. Agile retrospectives — the 2020 Scrum Guide

**(a)** Scrum's iteration (the Sprint) contains four formal events beyond the Sprint itself: Sprint Planning, Daily Scrum, Sprint Review, Sprint Retrospective.
**(b)** Reflection is *per-Sprint, never per-story*. The Guide is explicit: "The purpose of the Sprint Retrospective is to plan ways to increase quality and effectiveness. The Scrum Team inspects how the last Sprint went with regards to individuals, interactions, processes, tools, and their Definition of Done ... The Sprint Retrospective concludes the Sprint." Its output is forward-fed, cross-iteration: "The most impactful improvements are addressed as soon as possible. They may even be added to the Sprint Backlog for the next Sprint." There is no per-story or per-task retrospective anywhere in the Guide; within-sprint adaptation is continuous and lightweight instead ("The Daily Scrum is not the only time Developers are allowed to adjust their plan. They often meet throughout the day"). Note also the retrospective inspects the *process and the Definition of Done* — the verifier — which is Red Gate's cross-round criteria-evolution concern, not slice-level checking (slice-level checking in Scrum is the Definition of Done applied to each Increment). All quotes verbatim from the November 2020 Scrum Guide, https://scrumguides.org/scrum-guide.html.
**(c)** Schwaber & Sutherland, *The Scrum Guide*, November 2020, https://scrumguides.org/scrum-guide.html.

### 6. TDD red-green-refactor — Beck

**(a)** Canon TDD is five steps, of which three repeat per test: (1) write a test list once up front, then per item: (2) write one concrete test, (3) change the code to make it pass, (4) *optionally* refactor, (5) loop until the list is empty.
**(b)** In Beck's own canonical description, the closest thing to a reflection phase — refactor — is literally marked optional per cycle: "Optionally refactor to improve the implementation design" (Kent Beck, "Canon TDD," Dec 2023, https://tidyfirst.substack.com/p/canon-tdd — Beck wrote this post specifically because "folks out there don't agree on the definition of TDD. I made it as clear as possible in my book. I thought it was clear. Nope."). The load-bearing per-cycle structure is arm-then-work-then-judge: the test is written first and *must be seen to fail* (red — the criteria proven able to fail), the code change makes it and "all previous tests" pass (the pinned, accumulated verifier). Two further Canon TDD details map onto Red Gate directly: the Test List is written *before* coding as pure behavioral analysis ("Mistake: mixing in implementation design decisions"), i.e., criteria are armed before work; and discoveries during work go back onto the *list* ("adding items to the list as you discover them"), i.e., investigation findings are queued as future cycles, not expanded into the current one. Fowler's canonical bliki entry adds the sociological caveat: "The most common way that I hear to screw up TDD is neglecting the third step" (https://martinfowler.com/bliki/TestDrivenDevelopment.html) — refactoring matters, but two decades of practice show that an unenforced in-cycle quality phase is the step that silently disappears. That is an argument for making consolidation a *gated obligation* (a round type that must eventually run) rather than an honor-system phase in every cycle.
**(c)** Kent Beck, "Canon TDD," https://tidyfirst.substack.com/p/canon-tdd; Martin Fowler, "Test Driven Development," https://martinfowler.com/bliki/TestDrivenDevelopment.html; Kent Beck, *Test-Driven Development: By Example*, Addison-Wesley, 2002.

## Agent-loop literature (arXiv primary sources)

### 7. ReAct — Yao et al. 2022 (arXiv 2210.03629)

**(a)** Two interleaved move types per step: a reasoning trace and an action (with an environment observation returned). There is no fixed per-iteration phase count at all — "we explore the use of LLMs to generate both reasoning traces and task-specific actions in an interleaved manner ... reasoning traces help the model induce, track, and update action plans as well as handle exceptions, while actions allow it to interface with and gather additional information from external sources" (abstract, https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.03629).
**(b)** Investigation is not a phase; it is an *action* ("actions allow it to ... gather additional information"), and plan adjustment is not a phase; it is a *thought* emitted whenever needed. ReAct is the minimal do-work loop — in Red Gate terms, everything ReAct does lives inside stage 2 (the work), and ReAct itself contains no verifier at all. Its successors all add verification *outside* the ReAct actor (see Reflexion, LATS).
**(c)** Yao et al., "ReAct: Synergizing Reasoning and Acting in Language Models," https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.03629.

### 8. Reflexion — Shinn et al. 2023 (arXiv 2303.11366)

**(a)** Per *episode*: act (a full trial), evaluate (external or internal feedback signal), reflect (verbal summary), then a fresh episode.
**(b)** **Verified: reflection sits after the episode, feeding memory into the next episode — it is not a phase inside the act loop.** The abstract: "Reflexion agents verbally reflect on task feedback signals, then maintain their own reflective text in an episodic memory buffer to induce better decision-making in subsequent trials." The introduction is even more explicit about the cross-episode placement: "Reflexion converts binary or scalar feedback from the environment into verbal feedback in the form of a textual summary, which is then added as additional context for the LLM agent in the next episode," reflection being "akin to how humans iteratively learn ... by reflecting on their previous failures in order to form an improved plan of attack for the next attempt" (https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.11366). Reflection is also *triggered by a failure verdict* — the evaluator runs first, and reflection is the mechanism that converts a red verdict into next-round context. This is precisely a cross-round mechanism keyed off the judge stage, not a mandatory per-step phase.
**(c)** Shinn et al., "Reflexion: Language Agents with Verbal Reinforcement Learning," NeurIPS 2023, https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.11366.

### 9. Self-Refine — Madaan et al. 2023 (arXiv 2303.17651)

**(a)** Three moves per iteration: generate, feedback, refine — "generate an initial output using an LLM; then, the same LLM provides feedback for its output and uses it to refine itself, iteratively" (https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.17651).
**(b)** Feedback *is* in every iteration, but note two things. First, the phase count is three, and the third phase is the *next* round's work, so the steady-state loop is work→judge→work→judge — structurally Red Gate's stages 2–3 with the criteria left implicit. Second, the judge is the same model that did the work ("a single LLM as the generator, refiner and the feedback provider") with no pinned criteria — and that is exactly the configuration later shown to be unreliable: Huang et al. found that "LLMs struggle to self-correct their responses without external feedback, and at times, their performance even degrades after self-correction" ("Large Language Models Cannot Self-Correct Reasoning Yet," https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.01798). Self-Refine works on tasks with soft, preference-shaped quality ("hard-to-define goals, such as enhancing program readability"); it is not evidence that an unarmed, non-independent reflection phase helps on tasks with checkable correctness.
**(c)** Madaan et al., "Self-Refine: Iterative Refinement with Self-Feedback," NeurIPS 2023, https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.17651.

### 10. CRITIC — Gou et al. 2023 (arXiv 2305.11738)

**(a)** Two moves per iteration after the initial output: verify with external tools, then correct — "CRITIC interacts with external tools like search engines and code interpreters to verify the desired aspects of an initial output and subsequently amends the output based on the critiques from the verification. This verify-then-correct process can be repeated" (https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11738).
**(b)** Critique is per-iteration but the paper's core finding is about *what makes critique count*: "our research highlights the crucial importance of external feedback in promoting the ongoing self-improvement of LLMs" (abstract). CRITIC is the agent-loop rediscovery of the independent-judge requirement — the critique phase only earns its cost because it is grounded in tools the actor cannot argue with. It supports hardening Red Gate's stage 3 (the judge must be external/tool-grounded), not adding a self-directed Reflect phase.
**(c)** Gou et al., "CRITIC: Large Language Models Can Self-Correct with Tool-Interactive Critiquing," ICLR 2024, https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11738.

### 11. LATS — Zhou et al. 2023 (arXiv 2310.04406)

**(a)** LATS wraps ReAct-style acting in Monte Carlo Tree Search: per node, the MCTS operations (selection, expansion, evaluation, simulation, backpropagation, plus reflection on failed trajectories). It "integrate[s] Monte Carlo Tree Search into LATS to enable LMs as agents, along with LM-powered value functions and self-reflections for proficient exploration," with "the incorporation of an environment for external feedback" as "a key feature" (https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.04406).
**(b)** Reflection in LATS is inherited from Reflexion and attached to *failed trajectories* feeding future search, and evaluation is a value function over completed branches — both are search-control machinery *around* the act loop, not phases inside each act. Structurally: variance in what-happens-next lives in which *node type* the search executes (expand more vs. evaluate vs. backtrack) — the search-tree analogue of putting variance in round types rather than in a longer fixed per-step phase list.
**(c)** Zhou et al., "Language Agent Tree Search Unifies Reasoning, Acting, and Planning in Language Models," ICML 2024, https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.04406.

### 12. Tree of Thoughts — Yao et al. 2023 (arXiv 2305.10601)

**(a)** Per step: generate candidate thoughts, self-evaluate them, and let a search algorithm choose/backtrack — ToT "enables exploration over coherent units of text ('thoughts') ... considering multiple different reasoning paths and self-evaluating choices to decide the next course of action, as well as looking ahead or backtracking when necessary" (https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.10601).
**(b)** Evaluation is per-step, but as a *search heuristic* (which branch to pursue), not as reflection on completed work; there is no reflect phase. The deliberate/System-2 behavior the paper is after comes from the *search structure over states*, not from adding phases to each state's processing.
**(c)** Yao et al., "Tree of Thoughts: Deliberate Problem Solving with Large Language Models," NeurIPS 2023, https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.10601.

### 13. Anthropic, "Building effective agents" (Dec 2024)

**(a)** The evaluator-optimizer workflow is exactly two roles per loop: "one LLM call generates a response while another provides evaluation and feedback in a loop" — used "when we have clear evaluation criteria, and when iterative refinement provides measurable value" (https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-effective-agents). Their autonomous-agent pattern is likewise minimal: "typically just LLMs using tools based on environmental feedback in a loop," gaining "'ground truth' from the environment at each step."
**(b)** No reflection or investigation phase appears in any of the six catalogued patterns; the piece's repeated design pressure runs the other way: "we recommend finding the simplest solution possible, and only increasing complexity when needed," "you should consider adding complexity only when it demonstrably improves outcomes," and principle 1 of 3: "Maintain simplicity in your agent's design." Evaluator-optimizer is a two-party generate/judge split with criteria as precondition — Red Gate's stages 2–3, with stage 1 (clear criteria) stated as the *fitness condition* for using the pattern at all.
**(c)** Schluntz & Zhang, "Building effective agents," Anthropic, Dec 19 2024, https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-effective-agents.

## Evidence on the cost of mandatory per-iteration phases

- **Self-inspection phases lose to plain sampling at equal token cost.** Mirzaei (2026) re-ran seven reasoning-enhancement methods as a designed experiment, counting "every token a method generates, including the tokens spent on critiques, reflections, debate turns and checking," against repeated sampling at each method's own measured cost: "No method is reliably better than repeated sampling at equal cost in any setting. Ten are reliably worse, all of them methods in which the model inspects its own output ... Every one of the 18 comparisons involving self-inspection comes out negative" ("Sample More, Reflect Less: Self-Refine and Reflexion Lose to Repeated Sampling at Equal Token Cost, from 1.5B to 7B," https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.28576; small open models, math benchmarks — scope caveat applies, but the accounting method is the point: a reflect phase must beat spending the same tokens on more attempts).
- **Intrinsic self-correction can be worse than nothing.** Huang et al.: without external feedback "LLMs struggle to self-correct their responses ... and at times, their performance even degrades after self-correction" (https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.01798). A mandatory Reflect phase whose judge is the actor itself buys cost plus risk.
- **Uncalibrated extra thinking is measurably expensive.** Chen et al. document "overthinking ... where excessive computational resources are allocated for simple problems with minimal benefit"; on "what is 2 plus 3?" o1-like models "consumed 1,953% more tokens than conventional models to reach the same answer" ("Do NOT Think That Much for 2+3=?," https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.21187). Fixed per-iteration deliberation phases are exactly uncalibrated extra thinking.
- **Process literature: unenforced phases evaporate, enforced ones are rationed.** Fowler on TDD: "The most common way that I hear to screw up TDD is neglecting the third step" (https://martinfowler.com/bliki/TestDrivenDevelopment.html). Scrum's answer to ceremony cost is cadence rationing — one timeboxed retrospective per Sprint ("timeboxed to a maximum of three hours for a one-month Sprint"), events existing "to create regularity and to minimize the need for meetings not defined in Scrum" (https://scrumguides.org/scrum-guide.html). Polya observes that even good students skip Looking Back the moment it is unenforced (§13, cited above).

## Bottom line

**In mature loop designs, reflection lives between iterations, not inside every one.** The pattern across fourteen sources is consistent once each cycle's labels are decomposed into functions:

1. **Every source that reliably produces verified work has the same in-iteration skeleton Red Gate already has: criteria first, work, then a check the worker cannot self-satisfy.** PDSA's Plan defines success metrics before Do and Study compares against them; TDD writes the failing test first and the accumulated suite is the pinned verifier; CRITIC's per-iteration critique only works because it is grounded in external tools; Anthropic's evaluator-optimizer is two roles gated on pre-existing "clear evaluation criteria." Where the checking party is the actor itself with no pinned criteria (Self-Refine), the configuration is the one Huang et al. showed degrading performance on checkable tasks.
2. **The reflective/adaptive function is, in every mature design, either (i) the judge stage itself, (ii) a cross-iteration mechanism keyed off the judge's verdict, or (iii) a rationed separate event — never an extra mandatory phase inside each iteration.** Deming's Act adjusts theory for the next cycle; Reflexion — the flagship "reflection" agent paper — puts reflection *after* the episode, in an episodic memory buffer, "for subsequent trials," triggered by an evaluation; Scrum forbids nothing per-story and mandates exactly one timeboxed retrospective per Sprint whose output lands in the *next* Sprint's backlog; Beck marks per-cycle refactoring "Optionally" and instead queues discoveries onto the test list as future cycles; LATS reflects on failed trajectories to steer future search. Kolb is the lone in-cycle-reflection source, and his cycle produces learning rather than verified work — it describes what a gate should *extract from* a round, not what a round is.
3. **Investigation follows the same pattern: it is either an action available inside the work stage (ReAct, CRITIC's tool calls, Scrum's continuous refinement) or a dedicated iteration type / standing posture (Polya's Understand phase per problem, OODA's continuous Observe-Orient, TDD's one-time Test List, orientation rounds), never a mandatory per-iteration phase.**
4. **Mandatory phases carry measured cost and documented decay.** Token-matched, self-inspection phases lose to repeated sampling in all 18 comparisons tested (arXiv 2607.28576); uncalibrated deliberation costs ~20x tokens on easy inputs (arXiv 2412.21187); and the process record shows unenforced phases silently vanish (Fowler on refactor, Polya on Looking Back) while enforced ones survive only by being rationed to a coarser cadence (Scrum's per-Sprint retrospective).

**Implication for Red Gate:** keep the 3-stage round. Adding Reflect/Plan/Investigate as per-round phases would (a) duplicate function — verification-flavored reflection is already stage 3, planning already lives in stage 1 plus the plan round type, investigation is already the scout round type and an available move inside stage 2; (b) contradict the placement every mature source chose; and (c) buy the measured costs above. What the prior art *does* endorse strengthening, if anything, are the mechanisms Red Gate already locates outside the stages: a Reflexion-style obligation that a red verdict must produce a durable verbal artifact consumed by the next round's stage 1 (cross-round memory keyed off the judge); a Deming/Scrum-style cadence rule ensuring consolidation rounds actually recur rather than remaining optional the way Beck's refactor and Polya's Looking Back are; and CRITIC/Huang-style hardening that the stage-3 judge stays external and tool-grounded. Variance belongs in round types and gate obligations; the per-round phase count is settled prior art.
